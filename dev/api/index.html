API reference

Polytopes and warmup

PolytopeSamplers.Polytope — Type
Polytope(lower, upper; A, b, C, d)

The convex polytope of points x with lower .<= x .<= upper, A * x .<= b and C * x .== d. Bounds may be infinite as long as the constraints bound the polytope. A coordinate with lower[i] == upper[i] is fixed at that value.

A, b, C and d default to no constraints and must be finite. Constraints of the form L * x .>= f are A = -L, b = -f. Bounds of magnitude 1e20 or more are infinite, as for HiGHS.

isempty(P) solves a linear program to check whether P has any points, and x in P checks a point (see isfeasible).

Examples

julia> P = Polytope([0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0]; A = [1.0 1.0], b = [1.0])  # a triangle
Polytope in 2 variables with 1 inequalities and 0 equalities

julia> [0.2, 0.3] in P
true

julia> isempty(P)
false

PolytopeSamplers.warmup — Function
warmup([rng], P::Polytope; extremes = false, rounding = :ellipsoid, prune = true,
       optimizer = HiGHS.Optimizer, atol = 1e-8)

Prepare P for sampling and return a Warmup to sample from.

With extremes = false (the default), solve one linear program for the largest ball inside P in the slice of C * x .== d and the fixed coordinates, as in multiphase Monte Carlo sampling (mmcs). A polytope with room for a ball of positive radius has no other equalities. If the ball has radius below atol, one more linear program finds the inequalities that hold with equality over P (Freund, Roundy & Todd 1985), and the ball is found again in their slice. If it is still that small, P is thinner than atol in some direction, and warmup falls back to extremes = true, which measures such directions.

With extremes = true, solve 2n linear programs, minimizing and maximizing each of the n coordinates. The extreme points locate the constraints that hold with equality over all of P: a coordinate whose range is within atol of zero, or an inequality whose slack is within atol of zero at every extreme point. Chains move only in the directions that keep these equalities and C * x .== d, from a center that satisfies every constraint: the mean of the extreme points, with the equalities held at their values there.

atol is in the units of the variables. A polytope only atol wide in some direction is treated as flat in it: its samples are held at the center's value there, an error of at most atol. A coordinate whose bounds are within atol of each other is fixed at their midpoint. The default of 1e-8, as in PolytopeSamplerMatlab, is ten times the feasibility tolerance that warmup sets for HiGHS (1e-9), so only equalities that hold over the polytope are fixed; directions as thin as 1e-7 are sampled. With a larger atol, warmup warns when it fixes a direction wider than 1e-8. The coordinates that do not vary are listed by fixed in the result of sample_until_converged. The 2n programs cost far more for large n: 58 s for n = 200 with 1000 constraints, against 1.3 s for the whole warmup with extremes = false. ACHR uses the extreme points, and solves the 2n programs on first use when the warmup was built without them.

rounding changes coordinates so that the polytope is close to round, which makes every sampler take long steps in thin directions:

  • :ellipsoid (or true): map the maximum-volume inscribed ellipsoid to the unit ball, repeatedly, until it is nearly a ball. Each round factors dense matrices of size the number of constraints.
  • :samples: draw billiard-walk samples and map their principal axes to equal lengths, repeatedly, as in multiphase Monte Carlo sampling. Runs one chain per thread and uses rng, so a seeded result depends on Threads.nthreads().
  • :none (or false).

With prune = true (the default), solve one linear program per finite bound and inequality, and leave out of G and h each one whose removal leaves the polytope unchanged. These include constraints that are tight only on a lower-dimensional face, such as a bound that the other constraints reach at a single vertex. Each sampler step costs time in proportion to the number of constraints, and :ellipsoid rounding factors matrices of that size. On a food web with 282 variables, pruning removes 502 of 796 constraints and the warmup takes 6.4 s instead of 16.2 s. The programs are slow when A is dense: with 200 variables and 1000 dense inequalities they took 90 times as long as the rest of the warmup, so pass prune = false for such polytopes. w.polytope keeps every constraint, and w.removed counts those left out.

warmup first rescales the variables by geometric-mean scaling, as PolytopeSamplerMatlab does, so that the constraint coefficients of each variable are near 1, and solves its linear programs and rounding in the scaled variables. Without it, polytopes whose coefficients span many orders of magnitude, such as food webs with biomasses from 1e-3 to 1e3, gave the solver inaccurate points. The result is mapped back to P, and atol stays in the units of the variables.

Throws an ArgumentError when P is empty or unbounded; with extremes = false one more linear program checks boundedness. A linear program that stops for another reason raises an error naming its status. After rounding, warmup warns when the polytope is wider than 1e9 in some coordinate, as one bounded only by a very large bound is (the check of PolytopeSamplerMatlab).

optimizer is any JuMP optimizer for linear programs. warmup sets the feasibility tolerances of HiGHS to 1e-9 and warns for other solvers, whose tolerances should be set through optimizer_with_attributes; each program stops after 600 s unless a time limit is set there. Each call builds its own models, so separate tasks may call warmup at the same time.

PolytopeSamplers.Warmup — Type
Warmup

A polytope in the coordinates that samplers move in, built by warmup. Points are x = center + transform * z for z in {z : G z ≤ h}, where z has one coordinate per direction in which the polytope has width and the rows of G have unit norm.

  • polytope: the Polytope sampled.
  • points: the distinct extreme points of each coordinate, one per column.
  • center: the point z = 0, the center of the last inscribed ellipsoid when rounded, otherwise the center of the largest inscribed ball, or the mean of points with extremes = true.
  • transform: the n × k matrix taking z to x - center. Its columns are orthonormal when not rounded.
  • G, h: the constraints of P in z, leaving out those that are constant on the slice.
  • zpoints: points in z coordinates.
  • radius: the radius of the largest ball in {z : G z ≤ h}, near 1 when rounded.
  • rounded: whether transform rounds the polytope.
  • removed: the number of redundant bounds and inequalities left out of G and h.

With warmup(P; extremes = false) (the default), points and zpoints have no columns; ACHR computes the extreme points when a chain first needs them.

PolytopeSamplers.ranges — Function
ranges(P::Polytope; optimizer = HiGHS.Optimizer)
ranges(w::Warmup)

The smallest and largest value of each coordinate over the polytope, as the vectors (lower, upper), from the 2n linear programs that minimize and maximize each coordinate. Metabolic modeling calls this flux variability analysis. A coordinate with lower[i] == upper[i] is fixed by the constraints, and a narrow range means that the constraints nearly determine it.

For a warmup the extreme points of warmup(P; extremes = true) give the ranges without further programs. Otherwise the programs are solved on first use and kept in the warmup for later calls and for ACHR chains.

The ranges of samples lie inside these and can fall well short of them in thin corners of the polytope.

Examples

julia> lower, upper = ranges(Polytope([0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0]; A = [2.0 1.0], b = [1.0]));

julia> upper
2-element Vector{Float64}:
 0.5
 1.0

Targets

PolytopeSamplers.Target — Type
Target(w::Warmup, ℓ; gradient = nothing)
Target(P::Polytope, ℓ; gradient = nothing, kwargs...)

The distribution on a polytope with density proportional to exp(ℓ(x)), a model for sample and sample_until_converged. Given a Polytope, the other keyword arguments go to warmup.

ℓ is a function of the vector x of the polytope's variables, evaluated at points clamped to the polytope's bounds, or an object implementing the LogDensityProblems.jl interface (logdensity, dimension, capabilities, and logdensity_and_gradient for gradients). For a function, pass gradient = (g, x) -> ..., which fills g with ∇ℓ(x), to use CRHMC. ℓ is called from every chain, on separate threads when Julia has them.

A log density of +Inf or NaN counts as outside the support, as for a density that diverges at a face. HitAndRun and CoordinateHitAndRun draw each point on the chord from the density along it by slice sampling, so they leave the target invariant for any ℓ (GaussianTarget and ExponentialTarget are drawn exactly on chords); DikinWalk and VaidyaWalk add ℓ to their Metropolis ratio; CRHMC adds -ℓ to its potential and needs the gradient. BilliardWalk and ACHR sample only the uniform distribution.

Examples

P = Polytope(zeros(3), ones(3); C = ones(1, 3), d = [1.0])  # the simplex
# Dirichlet(2, 3, 4) on the simplex
target = Target(P, x -> sum((a - 1) * log(xi) for (a, xi) in zip((2, 3, 4), x));
                gradient = (g, x) -> (g .= ((2, 3, 4) .- 1) ./ x))
r = sample_until_converged(Xoshiro(1), target, CRHMC())

PolytopeSamplers.GaussianTarget — Function
GaussianTarget(w::Warmup, μ, Σ; whiten = false)
GaussianTarget(P::Polytope, μ, Σ; whiten = false, kwargs...)

The Gaussian distribution with mean μ and covariance Σ (a positive definite matrix) restricted to the polytope: a Target with ℓ(x) = -(x - μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x - μ) / 2. Given a Polytope, the other keyword arguments go to warmup.

On a chord the density is a truncated normal, which HitAndRun and CoordinateHitAndRun draw exactly, without slice sampling. The other samplers that take densities use ℓ and its gradient as for any target.

With whiten = true, the coordinates of the warmup are stretched in each direction where the Gaussian is narrower than the polytope's inscribed ball, until it is as wide as the ball, as hops samples truncated Gaussians in coordinates whitened by Σ. Use it for a narrow, correlated Gaussian that lies mostly inside the polytope: with correlation 0.99 on [-10, 10]², CoordinateHitAndRun reached a minimum ESS of 7797 from 4 chains of 2000 sweeps whitened, against 67 without. Where the constraints shape the target more than the Gaussian does it is slower: on a food web with 282 variables and σ a fifth of each coordinate's range, the minimum ESS from 4 chains of 20 000 sweeps was 729 whitened against 1019 without, so it is off by default.

Examples

box = Polytope(fill(-1.0, 2), fill(1.0, 2))
t = GaussianTarget(box, [0.5, 0.0], [1.0 0.8; 0.8 1.0])
r = sample_until_converged(Xoshiro(1), t, CoordinateHitAndRun())
source

Sampling

StatsBase.sample — Method
sample([rng], w::Warmup, method, n; discard_initial = 0, thinning = 1, progress = true)
sample([rng], w::Warmup, method, MCMCThreads(), n, nchains; kwargs...)
sample([rng], t::Target, method, args...; kwargs...)
sample([rng], P::Polytope, method, args...; extremes = false, rounding = :ellipsoid,
       optimizer = HiGHS.Optimizer, kwargs...)

Draw n points from the uniform distribution on a polytope, or from the density of a Target, returned as the columns of a matrix; with MCMCThreads(), MCMCSerial() or MCMCDistributed(), draw nchains chains and return a vector of matrices. method is CoordinateHitAndRun, BilliardWalk, HitAndRun or ACHR.

Every chain starts at a uniform random point of the largest ball around the warmup center inside the polytope, so the chains of an ensemble start apart, as R̂ in diagnostics assumes; pass initial_params, a point of the polytope (a vector of points, one per chain, with an ensemble), to choose the start. DikinWalk, VaidyaWalk and CRHMC move a start on the boundary toward the warmup center until it is strictly inside, as their barrier is infinite there. The chain then discards discard_initial steps and keeps every thinning-th point. Consecutive points are correlated; check the effective sample size with diagnostics. Given a Polytope, sample calls warmup once for all chains with the given extremes and rounding. To run chains until they converge, see sample_until_converged.

With MCMCChains.jl loaded, chain_type = Chains returns an MCMCChains.Chains (one for all chains of an ensemble), with parameters named x[1], x[2], … or by param_names, and the statistics of each step (AbstractMCMC.getstats, such as is_accept or n_reflections) in its internals section. AbstractMCMC.steps and callbacks receive each point as a transition with fields x and stats.

This is AbstractMCMC.sample (also StatsBase.sample); its other keyword arguments, such as initial_state and callback, apply. A chain continues from a copy of initial_state, so the state passed is left as it was and may be passed to several chains.

Examples

P = Polytope([0.0, 0.0], [1.0, 1.0]; A = [1.0 1.0], b = [1.0])  # a triangle
X = sample(Xoshiro(1), P, HitAndRun(), 1000; discard_initial = 100, thinning = 10)
chains = sample(Xoshiro(1), P, ACHR(), MCMCThreads(), 1000, 4; progress = false)
diagnostics(chains)
X = sample(Xoshiro(1), P, HitAndRun(), 1000; initial_params = [0.1, 0.1])

PolytopeSamplers.sample_until_converged — Function
sample_until_converged([rng], w::Warmup, method = BilliardWalk(); target_ess = 1000,
                       max_rhat = 1.01, nchains = 4, n = 1000, max_n = 1_000_000,
                       thinning = 1, multivariate = false)
sample_until_converged([rng], t::Target, method = HitAndRun(); kwargs...)

Run nchains chains, on the uniform distribution of a Warmup or the density of a Target, and lengthen them until the second half of the chains has a smallest effective sample size of at least target_ess and a largest R̂ below max_rhat over the varying coordinates; the first half is discarded as burn-in (Gelman & Rubin 1992). With multivariate = true, the multivariate R̂ of the chains (see diagnostics) must also be below max_rhat; it grows with the number of coordinates, so above about 20 varying coordinates this needs an ESS of many thousands.

The first round keeps n points per chain. Each later round sets the kept length from the effective samples per point of the last one, to 1.2 times the length that reaches target_ess, at most twice the last length (as volesti's mmcs extrapolates its run from the ESS), and doubles it while R̂ is too high. Rounds stop once max_n points per chain are kept, counted at the given thinning; the chains have then run twice as many, the first half as burn-in.

Only the kept half of each chain is stored. When it holds more than 20 points per effective sample (200 while the chains have fewer than 20 effective samples each), every other point is dropped and later rounds keep every second step, as PolytopeSamplerMatlab thins its stored chain; the same happens when the stored points would exceed 4 GiB. Long runs of slowly mixing chains thus keep a few thousand points per chain, about as informative as all of them.

Each chain starts at a random point of the largest ball around the warmup center inside the polytope and has its own random number generator, seeded from rng, so the result does not depend on the number of threads; chains run on separate threads when Julia has them.

Returns a named tuple: chains (one matrix per chain, a point per column), diagnostics (see diagnostics), n (points kept per chain), thinning (the steps between kept points, thinning times the automatic thinning), converged and fixed, the coordinates that do not vary on the slice. converged covers the varying coordinates only; fixed lists the others, which warmup holds at its center. A warning is logged when the chains have not converged by max_n.

source
PolytopeSamplers.mmcs — Function
mmcs([rng], P::Polytope; target_ess = 1000, optimizer = HiGHS.Optimizer, kwargs...)

Multiphase Monte Carlo sampling (Chalkis et al. 2021): prepare P with one linear program and rounding from billiard-walk samples (warmup(rng, P; extremes = false, rounding = :samples)), then run billiard-walk chains until they reach target_ess with R̂ below 1.01 (sample_until_converged, which takes the other keyword arguments).

Returns the named tuple of sample_until_converged with the warmup added.

This differs from the volesti implementation in its stopping rule: volesti sums the ESS of single chains across phases, including the rounding phases, and restarts each window of 100 points near the center; here the rounding samples are discarded and several chains run until R̂ also shows they agree.

Chalkis, A., Fisikopoulos, V., Tsigaridas, E. & Zafeiropoulos, H. (2021). Geometric algorithms for sampling the flux space of metabolic networks. 37th International Symposium on Computational Geometry (SoCG 2021), 21:1–21:16. https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2021.21

PolytopeSamplers.diagnostics — Function
diagnostics(chains; kind = :bulk, multivariate = false)

Effective sample size and split-R̂ of every coordinate, from one chain (a matrix with one point per column) or several chains of equal length (a vector of such matrices), as returned by sample.

Returns a named tuple:

  • ess, rhat: one value per coordinate, NaN for coordinates that are constant over every chain, such as fixed coordinates. rhat is the rank R̂, the larger of the R̂ of the rank-normalized points (bulk) and of their distances from the median (tail), which also flags chains that agree in location but differ in spread.
  • mean, std: the mean and standard deviation of each coordinate over all chains.
  • mcse_mean, mcse_std: their Monte Carlo standard errors, which account for the correlation between points; report estimates with these as error bars.
  • min_ess, max_rhat: the smallest ESS and the largest R̂ over the varying coordinates.
  • multivariate_rhat: with multivariate = true, the multivariate R̂ of Brooks & Gelman (1998) of the split chains, the largest R̂ of any linear combination of the coordinates; NaN otherwise. It flags chains that disagree along a direction such as x₁ - x₂ while every coordinate agrees. It costs a covariance matrix of the coordinates. It grows with the number of coordinates p even for independent draws: with an ESS near 3500 it was 1.007 for 10 coordinates, 1.03 for 50 and 1.10 for 200, so a cutoff of 1.01 suits a few coordinates or a large ESS.

kind sets the ESS: :bulk measures the center of each marginal and :tail its 5% and 95% quantiles (Vehtari et al. 2021). A common rule is to run until max_rhat < 1.01 and min_ess is at least 100 per chain. Computed with MCMCDiagnosticTools.jl.

Brooks, S. P. & Gelman, A. (1998). General methods for monitoring convergence of iterative simulations. Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics 7, 434–455. https://doi.org/10.1080/10618600.1998.10474787

Vehtari, A., Gelman, A., Simpson, D., Carpenter, B. & Bürkner, P.-C. (2021). Rank-normalization, folding, and localization: an improved R̂ for assessing convergence of MCMC. Bayesian Analysis 16, 667–718. https://doi.org/10.1214/20-BA1221

PolytopeSamplers.volume — Function
volume([rng], P::Polytope; error = 0.1, nchains = 4, n = 500, max_n = 1_000_000, kwargs...)
volume([rng], w::Warmup; error = 0.1, nchains = 4, n = 500, max_n = 1_000_000)

Estimate the volume of a polytope by multiphase Monte Carlo with cooling balls (Chalkis, Emiris & Fisikopoulos 2023). Given a Polytope, the other keyword arguments go to warmup. For a polytope with equalities, this is the k-dimensional volume of the slice it lies on, in the units of its variables; a single point has volume 1.

In the coordinates z of the warmup, the polytope K is cut by balls B(ρ₁) ⊃ B(ρ₂) ⊃ … centered at z = 0, chosen so that each body K ∩ B(ρᵢ₊₁) holds about an eighth of the volume of K ∩ B(ρᵢ), down to a last ball of which 10–15% lies in K. The volume is the product of the volume of the last ball, the share of it inside K, estimated from independent uniform points of the ball, and the ratios vol(K ∩ B(ρᵢ)) / vol(K ∩ B(ρᵢ₊₁)), each estimated from nchains coordinate hit-and-run chains on K ∩ B(ρᵢ), continued from the chains that chose the radius ρᵢ₊₁. A ratio is the share of the chains' states inside the next ball after each coordinate move. The chains first run 2n sweeps and keep the last n, then lengthen, keeping the second half, until R̂ of the distance from the center is below 1.01 and the Monte Carlo standard error of the ratio is small enough for error; each round extrapolates the length from the last one's standard error, at most doubling it. The variance error² of the log volume is split as in volesti: error² / 4L for the share of the last ball, whose independent points are cheap, and the rest equally over the L - 1 ratios from chains.

Returns a named tuple:

  • volume and log_volume;
  • log_stderr: the standard error of log_volume, about the relative standard error of volume, from the Monte Carlo standard errors of the phases, which are independent; at most error when every phase converges;
  • radii: the radii of the balls, from Inf (the polytope itself) to the last ball;
  • ratios: the estimated share of each body inside the next ball, then the share of the last ball inside the polytope;
  • converged: whether every phase reached its error before keeping max_n sweeps per chain (of 2max_n run);
  • warmup.

This adapts the annealing schedule of volesti's volume_cooling_balls and differs in its stopping rule: volesti stops each phase when a sliding window of running ratios varies little, which gives no standard error; here each phase runs until its Monte Carlo standard error, which accounts for the correlation of the chain, meets the target.

Chalkis, A., Emiris, I. Z. & Fisikopoulos, V. (2023). A practical algorithm for volume estimation based on billiard trajectories and simulated annealing. ACM Journal of Experimental Algorithmics 28. https://doi.org/10.1145/3584182

Examples

cube = Polytope(zeros(10), ones(10))
v = volume(Xoshiro(1), cube)
v.volume, v.log_stderr  # about 1

PolytopeSamplers.BilliardWalk — Type
BilliardWalk(; trajectory_length = nothing, max_reflections = nothing)

The billiard walk (Gryazina & Polyak 2014) in its accelerated form (Chalkis et al. 2021): each step draws a direction uniformly from the sphere and a length from the exponential distribution with mean trajectory_length, and moves that far, reflecting off the facets of the polytope. The chain is reversible with the uniform distribution as its stationary distribution, and crosses a rounded polytope in a few steps, as a trajectory is not stopped by the first facet it meets.

trajectory_length defaults to 2√k r in the coordinates of the warmup, for dimension k and inscribed radius r (Warmup.radius, near 1 after rounding), as in volesti. On a warmup built with rounding = :none the inscribed ball says little about the length of the polytope, so the default is raised to the largest distance between the ends of 10 + log k short walks from the center, as volesti's burn-in does, repeated with the new length until it grows by less than 10%. On the unrounded box [0, 100] × [0, 1]⁹ this gave R̂ 1.001 and 7 times the effective samples per second of 2√k r, which gave R̂ 1.40 after 4000 steps per chain. The walks use a fixed seed, so all chains of a warmup share the length.

A trajectory that makes more than max_reflections reflections (default 1000k) is abandoned and the chain stays put for that step. getstats reports is_abandoned, and a chain warns once when more than 5% of its steps after the first 100 were abandoned. A reflection costs O(m) for m constraints, as the rates G v are updated from the precomputed G Gᵀ, an m × m matrix that the chains on one Warmup share.

Gryazina, E. & Polyak, B. (2014). Random sampling: billiard walk algorithm. European Journal of Operational Research 238, 497–504. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2014.03.041

Chalkis, A., Fisikopoulos, V., Tsigaridas, E. & Zafeiropoulos, H. (2021). Geometric algorithms for sampling the flux space of metabolic networks. 37th International Symposium on Computational Geometry (SoCG 2021), 21:1–21:16. https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.SoCG.2021.21

PolytopeSamplers.CoordinateHitAndRun — Type
CoordinateHitAndRun(; moves = nothing, scan = :cyclic)

Coordinate hit-and-run: each move resamples one coordinate of the warmup uniformly on its chord. On a rounded warmup this is CHRR (Haraldsdóttir et al. 2017), the default sampler of the COBRA Toolbox. For a Target, each move draws from the density along the chord by slice sampling.

With scan = :cyclic (the default, as in hopsy) the coordinates are taken in turn; with scan = :random each is chosen at random and the chain is reversible. Both keep the target stationary; the cyclic scan gave 1.3 to 1.8 times the effective samples per move on the polytopes of the test suite.

A move costs O(m) for m constraints, as the slacks are updated in place. Each step of the chain makes moves moves (default: the dimension k of the warmup), a sweep over the coordinates that costs about as much as one step of HitAndRun. hops and volesti count one coordinate move as a step, which moves = 1 gives. Moves per effective sample grow as k².

A chain started exactly at a vertex never moves when no coordinate axis points into the polytope there, as on a simplex; sample and sample_until_converged start chains inside the largest ball around the warmup center, away from vertices, unless given initial_params.

Haraldsdóttir, H. S., Cousins, B., Thiele, I., Fleming, R. M. T. & Vempala, S. (2017). CHRR: coordinate hit-and-run with rounding for uniform sampling of constraint-based models. Bioinformatics 33, 1741–1743. https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btx052

PolytopeSamplers.HitAndRun — Type
HitAndRun()

Hit-and-run (Smith 1984): each step draws a direction uniformly from the unit sphere in the coordinates of the warmup and moves to a uniform point on the chord of the polytope along it. The chain is reversible, with the uniform distribution as its stationary distribution. A step costs O(mk) for m constraints in k dimensions. For a Target, the point on the chord is drawn from the density along it by slice sampling.

A step is one move along one direction, as in volesti, hops and the COBRA Toolbox. A step of CoordinateHitAndRun makes k moves, one per coordinate, at about the same cost, so a HitAndRun chain needs about k times as many steps to mix as well. On food web polytopes with 50 to 176 dimensions, sample_until_converged with HitAndRun stopped at max_n with R̂ from 1.3 to 2.9, while CoordinateHitAndRun converged in 0.2 to 3.7 s. Above a few dimensions, use CoordinateHitAndRun for a density and BilliardWalk for the uniform distribution, or give HitAndRun thinning = k.

Smith, R. L. (1984). Efficient Monte Carlo procedures for generating points uniformly distributed over bounded regions. Operations Research 32, 1296–1308. https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.32.6.1296

source
PolytopeSamplers.CRHMC — Type
CRHMC(; initial_step_size = 0.2, effective_step_size = 1.0, max_ode_steps = 30,
      implicit_tol = 1e-5)

Constrained Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Kook, Lee, Shen & Vempala 2022): Hamiltonian dynamics in the metric of the log barrier, which slows the chain down only in the directions in which a facet is near, so it takes long steps through the whole polytope.

CRHMC writes the polytope as {x : A x = b, l ≤ x ≤ u} with a diagonal barrier metric projected onto A x = b, which keeps large sparse problems cheap. Here the dynamics run in the coordinates z of the Warmup: with slacks s = h - G z as the bounded variables, the projected metric is g(z) = Gᵀ S⁻² G, the Hessian of the log barrier of G z ≤ h, and the Hamiltonian is

H(z, p) = ½ log det g(z) + ½ pᵀ g(z)⁻¹ p,

minus the log density for a Target.

Each step refreshes the momentum partially (p ← √μ p + √(1 - μ) g^½ ξ with μ = 1 - h / effective_step_size), takes one step of the Strang splitting of the potential and kinetic parts, solving the kinetic part by the implicit midpoint rule with fixed-point iterations (up to max_ode_steps, to a local-norm tolerance implicit_tol), and accepts by a Metropolis filter on H; a rejection reverses the momentum. The step size h grows from 0.001 to initial_step_size over the first 10 effective steps, then shrinks by a factor of 1.1 whenever rejections, failed integrations or integration effort exceed their targets, as in the reference implementation. A step factors the k × k metric once or twice, O(mk² + k³) each, and each implicit iteration costs O(mk).

The metric is affine-invariant, so in exact arithmetic the chain behaves the same in any coordinates, but its condition number grows as the square of the polytope's aspect ratio, and badly scaled polytopes need the default rounding: on a rotated box with sides from 1e-3 to 1e3 without rounding, chains reached points 1e-6 from a facet where every integration failed, and stayed there.

A step whose implicit integration does not converge is rejected. As failures happen more often near the boundary, they bias the chain slightly toward the interior; the metric is refactored during slow integrations to keep them below about 0.1% of steps, where the bias was not detectable over 20 seeds.

Kook, Y., Lee, Y. T., Shen, R. & Vempala, S. S. (2022). Sampling with Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in a constrained space. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 35. https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01908

PolytopeSamplers.DikinWalk — Type
DikinWalk(; r = 0.5)

The Dikin walk (Kannan & Narayanan 2012): each step proposes a Gaussian move shaped by the Dikin ellipsoid of the log barrier at the current point, N(z, (r²/k) H(z)⁻¹) with H(z) = Gᵀ S⁻² G for slacks S, and accepts it by a Metropolis filter, so the chain has the uniform distribution as its stationary distribution, or the density of a Target, whose log density joins the Metropolis ratio. The walk is affine-invariant, so it needs no rounding, and it has a mixing bound of O(mk) steps from a warm start for m constraints in k dimensions, whatever the shape of the polytope. A step costs O(mk²). On rounded polytopes it needed 340 to 490 steps per effective sample, about 100 times the BilliardWalk; use it as an independent check, or when rounding is not available.

Kannan, R. & Narayanan, H. (2012). Random walks on polytopes and an affine interior point method for linear programming. Mathematics of Operations Research 37, 1–20. https://doi.org/10.1287/moor.1110.0519

PolytopeSamplers.VaidyaWalk — Type
VaidyaWalk(; r = 0.7)

The Vaidya walk (Chen, Dwivedi, Wainwright & Yu 2018): the DikinWalk with each constraint weighted by its leverage score plus k/m (Vaidya's volumetric barrier), and proposals N(z, (r²/√(km)) H(z)⁻¹). Its moves per effective sample grow as O(m^½ k^1.5) instead of O(mk), which helps when there are many more constraints than dimensions; a step costs about twice a Dikin step.

Chen, Y., Dwivedi, R., Wainwright, M. J. & Yu, B. (2018). Fast MCMC sampling algorithms on polytopes. Journal of Machine Learning Research 19, 1–86. https://jmlr.org/papers/v19/18-158.html

PolytopeSamplers.ACHR — Type
ACHR()

Artificial centering hit-and-run (Kaufman & Smith 1998), as in the COBRA Toolbox: each direction runs from the running mean of the chain to a random extreme point of a coordinate, so directions follow the shape of the polytope. The direction depends on the history through the mean, so the chain is only approximately Markov, and its distribution approaches uniform as the mean settles; on rounded polytopes it is the slowest method here. It samples only the uniform distribution.

A warmup built with extremes = false (the default) solves the 2n linear programs for the extreme points when the first ACHR chain starts, and its chains share them. As in the COBRA Toolbox, the optima of random linear objectives are added until there are 2k points for a slice of dimension k, so that the directions span the polytope when many coordinates reach their bounds at the same vertex.

Kaufman, D. E. & Smith, R. L. (1998). Direction choice for accelerated convergence in hit-and-run sampling. Operations Research 46, 84–95. https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.46.1.84

Supertype of the samplers: BilliardWalk, the hit-and-run methods (AbstractHitAndRun), DikinWalk, VaidyaWalk and CRHMC. All of them move in the coordinates of a Warmup, rounded by default.
